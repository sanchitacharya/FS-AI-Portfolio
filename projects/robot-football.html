01 · Context

What is STRIDE?

STRIDE is a university robot football team working with ROBOTIS OP3 humanoid robots. To play, a robot has to answer two questions all the time: where is everything else on the pitch, and where am I?

My work covers both. Each piece is built and tested in simulation first, then taken to the real robot and its camera.

02 · Project

Phase 1 · Simulation + real robot

Multi-object tracking

This is the first phase of STRIDE’s visual localisation project: building a ground-truth tracking system. Static cameras placed around the field are calibrated to the pitch and track the robots and the ball, giving their positions in field coordinates over time.

These positions are used only during development, to train and evaluate the robot’s own localisation. In a match the robot localises with just its onboard camera and motion, so the final system needs no external setup.

  • Simulation The basic concept: tracking one robot and one ball. The simulated camera can’t give 3D positions like the real OAK-D, so positions come from the Webots supervisor, the way the world was set up. It shows how the tracker works before moving to real detections.
  • Real robot Runs on live detections from an OAK-D depth camera: robots, the ball, and field line intersections, each with a 3D position. The field frame is anchored once an X-shaped line intersection is seen. Robots and the ball are then placed in field coordinates and fed into the same tracker. Detections with no valid depth are ignored.
  • What: track every robot and the ball from static cameras, in field coordinates, with stable IDs.
  • Why: accurate ground truth to train and evaluate the robot’s onboard localisation.
  • Now: basic concept working in simulation; real version running on OAK-D detections.
  • Next: track multiple robots on the real field, then use this system as the teacher for learning-based localisation on the robot.

03 · Project

First version · simulation

Odometry-based localization with visual landmarks

A walking humanoid is hard to localize. Leg odometry drifts, the camera shakes with every step, and a football pitch is symmetric, so the two ends look almost the same. Without a reliable pose, you can’t really use the ball position or any tracked robot.

The approach combines odometry with vision. The IMU gives a continuous heading, and goalposts act as visual landmarks at known positions on the field. The first goalpost the robot sees sets up the map. After that, heading is used to tell whether the goal in view is ours or the opponent’s, which gets around the symmetry problem. From that, the robot’s position is solved relative to the centre of the pitch. Once the robot is localized, the ball is placed on the same field map.

  • Problem: odometry drift on a walking robot, plus a symmetric pitch where the two ends look alike.
  • Approach: IMU heading for odometry, with goalposts as known visual landmarks to correct position.
  • Output: robot pose and ball position in pitch coordinates, shown live on an RViz field map.
  • Next: move to the real robot, add more field features such as line intersections, and hold the pose when no goalpost is in view.

04 · Tools

Stack (high level)

Both projects are ROS 2 nodes in C++. Simulation is used to iterate quickly, and the real OP3 robots with an OAK-D camera are the final test.

  • C++ · Eigen

  • Data association
